Safety

Lost? Stop and assess

Avoid turning uncertainty into a larger rescue problem.

  1. Stop moving, take a moment, and avoid rushing into unfamiliar terrain.
  2. Check for immediate hazards: cliffs, weather, rising water, cold, or active fire.
  3. Use an available phone, satellite messenger, or emergency beacon to request help if needed; share your location.
  4. If you cannot confidently retrace a short safe route, stay in a safe visible location rather than wandering.
  5. Conserve energy, insulate against weather, and use a whistle or visible signals to help searchers find you.

Safety: Do not follow streams or travel cross-country just because they appear to lead downhill; these routes can introduce steep terrain and hazards.

General backcountry preparedness guidance; seek local park-specific advice.
Safety

Before you leave: trip plan

Make sure someone knows where to look if you do not return.

  1. Tell a trusted person your route, destination, group members, vehicle location, and expected return time.
  2. Agree on the time they should contact authorities if you are overdue.
  3. Check official weather, closures, fire restrictions, daylight, and current trail conditions.
  4. Carry navigation, weather protection, extra supplies, and at least two ways to signal or communicate.
  5. Turn around early if conditions or your group’s capabilities change.

Safety: Offline maps are not automatically included in this app. Bring downloaded maps and a paper backup.

Safety

Lightning & thunderstorms

There is no safe outdoor location during a thunderstorm.

  1. At the first thunder, seek a substantial building or fully enclosed hard-topped vehicle.
  2. Avoid summits, ridgelines, isolated trees, open shelters, water, and conductive objects.
  3. Stay in shelter for at least 30 minutes after the last thunder.
  4. If no shelter exists, leave exposed high areas and keep moving toward proper shelter without creating additional terrain risk.
  5. If someone is struck, call emergency services; a lightning victim does not retain a charge and can be touched safely.

Safety: Crouching outdoors does not make you safe from lightning. A tent is not lightning shelter.

Safety

Cold, wet & wind exposure

Reduce heat loss before an uncomfortable situation becomes critical.

  1. Move out of wind and precipitation; use a shelter layer or rain protection.
  2. Replace wet clothing with dry insulating layers when practical.
  3. Insulate yourself from cold ground with a pad, backpack, or dry material.
  4. Eat and drink if alert and able to swallow; avoid alcohol.
  5. Get emergency help for confusion, loss of coordination, extreme drowsiness, or suspected severe hypothermia.

Safety: Suspected moderate or severe hypothermia needs urgent medical care and gentle handling. This guide does not replace first-aid training.

Safety

Heat exposure

Recognize heat illness early and reduce heat load.

  1. Stop activity and move to a cooler shaded area.
  2. Loosen excess clothing and cool skin with water and airflow.
  3. Drink water if fully alert and able to swallow.
  4. Seek emergency help immediately for confusion, collapse, seizure, or suspected heat stroke; initiate rapid cooling while awaiting help.

Safety: Heat stroke is a medical emergency.

Water

Boiling water safely

Reliable germ reduction when fuel and equipment are available.

  1. Use the safest available water source; avoid water suspected of chemical or fuel contamination.
  2. If cloudy, settle water or strain it through a clean cloth or coffee filter to remove visible particles.
  3. Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 full minute.
  4. At elevations above 6,500 ft (about 2,000 m), boil for 3 full minutes.
  5. Allow to cool and store in a clean, sanitized, covered container.

Safety: Boiling kills germs but DOES NOT remove fuels, toxic chemicals, or radioactive contamination. Use a different source when contamination is suspected.

Water

Filters and purification

Know what your water-treatment gear actually removes.

  1. Read your filter manufacturer’s instructions, service life, and freeze damage warnings.
  2. Choose clearer water and prefilter sediment if needed.
  3. Use an appropriate filter for bacteria and protozoa; many backpacking filters do not remove viruses.
  4. Where required, follow filtration with a manufacturer-approved disinfection step.
  5. Keep contaminated hoses and dirty containers away from treated water.

Safety: No single backpacking filter removes every hazard. Filters and disinfection do not reliably make chemically contaminated water safe.

Water

Water planning

Carry water and a fallback treatment method.

  1. Identify known water sources along the planned route before starting.
  2. Carry enough water for terrain, temperature, exertion, and delays; needs vary substantially.
  3. Keep reserve capacity and a treatment backup when practical.
  4. Avoid relying on seasonal streams that may be dry.
  5. Drink regularly and watch for worsening symptoms of dehydration or heat illness.

Safety: This entry does not prescribe a universal quantity because needs vary widely.

Shelter

Site selection for shelter

A dry, protected location is often more valuable than an elaborate structure.

  1. Stay clear of dead trees, unstable limbs, rockfall, avalanche paths, and flood-prone channels.
  2. Choose ground above drainage routes and away from exposed ridges.
  3. Use existing tent or tarp equipment; secure it for anticipated wind direction.
  4. Insulate from cold ground and manage condensation while maintaining ventilation.
  5. Do not use fuel-burning stoves, fires, or charcoal inside tents or enclosed shelters.

Safety: Carbon monoxide can be fatal even when an enclosed shelter feels ventilated.

Clothing and insulation layers

Control moisture, wind, and loss of body heat.

  2. Avoid prolonged contact with wet cotton in cold weather.
  3. Keep critical spare insulation in a waterproof bag.
  4. Vent layers before sweat soaks clothing during exertion.
  5. Prioritize dry socks, head coverage, hand protection, and sleeping insulation.

Compass basics

Understand your tool before depending on it.

  1. Hold the compass flat and away from metal or magnets.
  2. Identify the magnetic needle’s north-seeking end.
  3. Rotate the bezel to align the orienting arrow with the needle if using a baseplate compass.
  4. Use a map with an appropriate declination adjustment for your region and date.
  5. Practice following a known bearing in a safe area before a remote trip.

Safety: A compass points to magnetic north, not necessarily true north. Device magnetic sensors may be inaccurate.

Communication

Emergency signaling

Make yourself easier to locate.

  1. If safe, move to a visible location without risking a fall or further injury.
  2. Use an emergency communicator or phone to relay coordinates and circumstances.
  3. A whistle carries farther than shouting and uses less energy.
  4. Use repeating, conspicuous visual or audible signals to attract searchers.
  5. Conserve battery power and signal equipment for critical communications.

Safety: Signal conventions vary by country and setting. Never assume a phone flashlight or a trainer tone can reach rescuers.

Communication

Morse code and SOS

Learn the recognizable three-short, three-long, three-short distress pattern.

  1. In international Morse, a dot lasts one unit and a dash lasts three units.
  2. The pause between elements of one letter is one unit; between letters three units; between words seven units.
  3. SOS is ··· ——— ··· and can be repeated using a suitable light or audible signaling device.
  4. Practice hearing letters and sending steady timing using the Morse Lab.
  5. Use dedicated emergency communications whenever available; Morse does not replace a distress call.

Campcraft

Three useful camp knots

Know a few knots and practice them before you need them.

  1. Bowline: creates a fixed loop; practice tying and checking its proper form.
  2. Clove hitch: quick attachment to a post; can slip if not backed up or loaded appropriately.
  3. Taut-line hitch: adjustable guyline tension for tents and tarps.
  4. Inspect all knots and lines for slipping, abrasion, and changing loads.
  5. Never use general camp knots for climbing, rescue, lifting people, or life safety.

Fire

Wildfire awareness

Prevention and early evacuation come before fire tactics.

  1. Before departure, review local fire restrictions, closures, and active incident information.
  2. If you see smoke or fire, move away from the hazard and notify emergency authorities.
  3. Avoid drainages, canyons, and routes where fire or smoke could block your exit.
  4. Never attempt wildland fire suppression without training, equipment, and authority.
  5. Follow official evacuation directions promptly; do not delay to take photos or retrieve gear.

Safety: Fieldcraft is not an operational wildland firefighting guide. Refer trained responders to the current NWCG IRPG.

Fire

Campfire prevention

A small campfire can cause a large emergency.

  1. Confirm that fires are permitted on the day and at the specific site.
  2. Prefer a stove when allowed and practical; respect stove restrictions too.
  3. Use established fire rings where designated; maintain control at all times.
  4. Keep water and tools immediately available and never leave a flame unattended.
  5. Drown, stir, and check remaining ashes until completely cold before leaving.

Safety: Follow the most restrictive local rules. Do not build a campfire when conditions are unsafe.

Plan an edible garden

Begin with your site, season, and the foods you actually eat.

  1. Observe sunlight and water access before choosing beds or containers.
  2. Check local frost dates, elevation, and the growing needs of each crop; use them as estimates rather than guarantees.
  3. Sketch beds, walking paths, water sources, and planned crop locations.

Gardening

Soil, compost & safe amendments

Healthy soil supports plant growth; avoid guesswork with fertilizers.

  1. Test soil when possible for pH and nutrient needs using an appropriate lab or extension service.
  2. Add finished compost and organic material to improve soil condition according to crop needs.
  3. Avoid fresh manure on edible crops; follow established food safety waiting periods for manure use.
  4. Keep compost piles aerated and adequately moist; do not apply unfinished compost where it may contaminate harvests.
  5. Use fertilizer according to label directions and test results, especially near waterways.

Safety: Be alert to lead or other contaminants in soil near older buildings, roads, or former industrial uses.

Seeds, starts & transplanting

Match the crop to its growing season.

  1. Read seed packets for recommended soil temperature, planting depth, spacing, and days to maturity.
  2. Start appropriate crops indoors with sufficient light, clean containers, and a suitable seed-starting mix.
  3. Harden off indoor-grown seedlings gradually before transplanting outdoors.
  4. Plant cool-season and warm-season crops at appropriate times for your local conditions.
  5. Label varieties, record planting dates, and observe germination and transplant performance.

Watering, mulch & heat

Maintain steady moisture while reducing waste.

  1. Check soil moisture below the surface rather than using a fixed daily watering rule.
  2. Water at the root zone using drip irrigation or a soaker hose where practical.
  3. Mulch with suitable clean material to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.
  4. Adjust watering for heat, rainfall, containers, and plant development.
  5. Maintain drainage; waterlogged roots and saturated soil can harm plants.

Safety: Avoid splashing potentially contaminated water on harvestable produce.

Pest management without guesswork

Identify the problem before intervening.

  2. Distinguish beneficial insects from pests; identify the organism before using a treatment.
  3. Start with appropriate cultural or physical controls such as sanitation, row covers, or hand removal.
  4. Use pesticides only when justified, labeled for the crop and pest, and according to all label instructions.
  5. Record what you tried and whether it worked.

Preservation

Choose a safe preservation method

The food, acidity, equipment, and recipe determine the method.

  1. Identify the food and intended storage method before starting.
  2. For shelf-stable home canning, select a CURRENT tested recipe from USDA, NCHFP, or a university extension service.
  3. Low-acid foods such as plain vegetables, meat, poultry, and fish require proper pressure canning.
  4. Boiling-water canning is for suitable high-acid or properly acidified foods when directed by a tested recipe.
  5. Follow the tested recipe exactly, including acidity, jar size, headspace, process time, pressure, and altitude adjustment.

Safety: Do not substitute an electric multi-cooker, oven, open-kettle method, or inverted jars for approved canning equipment. This guide is an overview, not a processing recipe.

CDC — home-canned food and botulism Source online
Preservation

Pressure canning: safety essentials

Low-acid foods need a tested pressure-canning process.

  1. Use a purpose-built pressure canner appropriate for the tested recipe, not a small pressure cooker or electric multi-cooker.
  2. Inspect equipment, venting, dial gauge calibration needs, jars, seals, and manufacturer instructions before use.
  3. Use only a tested food-specific recipe and follow its preparation, jar size, pressure, altitude, and timing instructions.
  4. Never shorten processing or substitute a boiling-water bath for low-acid food.
  5. After processing, follow the manufacturer and tested-recipe cooling and seal-check procedures; never force-cool the canner.

Safety: Incorrect home canning can cause botulism. This app deliberately does not supply generic pressure/time values.

National Center for Home Food Preservation — recommended canners Source online
Preservation

Boiling-water canning: know the limits

Only use this method when a tested recipe explicitly permits it.

  1. Select a tested high-acid or properly acidified product recipe.
  2. Use the prescribed jar size, measured acid additions where required, and exact ingredient proportions.
  3. Use a proper canner and maintain the required water coverage and full processing boil per the tested instructions.
  4. Adjust processing time for your altitude using the recipe’s official table.
  5. Allow jars to cool undisturbed and check seals using tested post-processing guidance.

Safety: Plain green beans, corn, meat, soups, and other low-acid foods are NOT safe for boiling-water canning. Tomatoes require the acidification specified by tested recipes.

Preservation

Recipe selection & altitude check

A saved family recipe may not be a safe canning recipe.

  1. Find a current research-tested recipe for the exact product and style of pack.
  2. Check food type, added acid, salt or sugar requirements, approved substitutions, and specific jar size.
  3. Determine your canning location’s elevation before selecting the applicable process.
  4. Follow all venting, temperature, pressure, and time instructions when applicable.
  5. Record recipe source, publication/revision, altitude, process details, date, and batch number.

Safety: Do not invent processing times, alter low-acid recipe proportions, or use pressure-cooker cooking instructions as canning directions.

Preservation

Freezing garden produce

Freezing is often a flexible alternative to canning.

  1. Choose fresh, sound produce and wash using food-safe practices.
  2. Consult crop-specific extension guidance for whether blanching is recommended and for how long.
  3. Cool and drain blanched produce as directed, package in freezer-safe materials, and remove excess air.
  4. Label contents, preparation date, quantity, and any pre-treatment.
  5. Freeze promptly and maintain freezer temperature at 0°F / −18°C or colder.

Safety: Freezing preserves food but does not reliably kill all pathogens; thaw and prepare safely.

Preservation

Dehydrating fruits, vegetables & herbs

Drying requires food-specific preparation and adequate drying.

  1. Start with clean, sound food and identify a tested drying method for that food.
  2. Prepare uniform pieces and use recommended pre-treatments when called for.
  3. Follow food-specific temperature and dryness guidance from extension resources.
  4. Cool completely before packaging; inspect stored food for moisture and mold.
  5. Store in labeled, sealed containers under suitable cool, dry conditions.

Safety: Do not assume sun drying or air drying is safe for all foods. Meat jerky requires separate validated food-safety directions.

Preservation

Fermentation & pickling basics

Fermenting, refrigerator pickling, and shelf-stable canning are different processes.

  1. Use a current tested recipe for the exact fermented or pickled food.
  2. Measure the recipe’s salt, vinegar, and water accurately; do not dilute vinegar arbitrarily.
  3. Keep fermenting vegetables below the brine as the tested directions specify, using clean equipment.
  4. Follow recipe-specific fermentation temperature, monitoring, and refrigeration or processing directions.
  5. Refrigerator pickles must be refrigerated unless a tested shelf-stable canning recipe is followed.

Safety: Do not treat an untested ferment as shelf stable just because a lid seals.

Preservation

Pantry storage, labels & jar inspection

Keep an inventory and recognize unsafe canned food.

  1. Label each jar with contents, batch and processing date, and method or recipe source.
  2. Store sealed jars in a clean, cool, dark location protected from heat and freezing.
  3. Inspect for broken seals, leakage, bulging lids, spurting liquid, mold, or unusual appearance before use.
  4. Never taste suspicious canned food to determine whether it is safe.
  5. Follow CDC guidance for handling and disposal of suspected contaminated jars.

Safety: Botulinum toxin cannot reliably be detected by smell, taste, or appearance. A sealed lid alone does not prove safe processing.

Safety

Survival priorities: assess before acting

The first job is to avoid making an emergency worse.

  1. Stop; check for imminent dangers, injury, cold, heat, water, unstable ground, and approaching weather.
  2. Call or signal for rescue early when an emergency warrants it; provide coordinates or landmarks and nature of the emergency.
  3. Move only as needed to reach a safer nearby position; avoid unnecessary travel if lost.
  4. Address exposure: get out of wind and precipitation, add insulation, and avoid sweating through clothing.
  5. Manage drinkable water and ration effort rather than restricting drinking water.
  6. Make yourself findable with bright material, a whistle, and an appropriate signal.

Safety: The familiar “rule of threes” is only a mnemonic, not a medical or survival timer. Circumstances vary dramatically.

Safety

River and floodwater hazards

Fast, cold water can be more dangerous than it looks.

  1. Avoid entering swift, deep, discolored, or rising water.
  2. Check bridges and established crossings instead of improvising a ford.
  3. Do not step onto flooded roads, stream channels, or undercut banks.
  4. Watch for upstream storm activity that can trigger sudden rises even under clear skies.
  5. If a crossing is unsafe, turn back or wait in a secure location and notify your contact of the change.

Safety: Do not treat a trekking-pole river crossing as a beginner-safe technique. Seek expert instruction for river travel.

Safety

Injury response in the field

Protect the scene, summon help, and stay within your training.

  1. Check that the location is safe for you and the injured person.
  2. Call emergency services or activate a satellite emergency device for serious injury, altered consciousness, major bleeding, or breathing difficulties.
  3. Follow current first-aid training; avoid moving someone with suspected spine or major injury unless there is immediate danger.
  4. Keep the person sheltered from temperature extremes and monitor them while help is coming.
  5. Note the location, time, symptoms, and any changes to communicate to responders.

Safety: This is a preparedness overview, not a substitute for CPR, first aid, or wilderness first responder training.

Water

Choosing a water source

Better collection choices simplify treatment but do not eliminate it.

  1. Prefer the clearest accessible water away from camps, livestock, runoff, and industrial contamination.
  2. Avoid water with visible fuel, chemical contamination, unusual odor, or suspect algal blooms.
  3. Collect from a safer bank without entering dangerous currents or steep terrain.
  4. Settle cloudy water or prefilter through a clean cloth before the treatment your equipment requires.
  5. Treat even clear-looking backcountry water before drinking.

Safety: Boiling and ordinary backcountry filtration do not remove hazardous chemicals or toxins. Find a different source.

Water

Field water treatment checklist

Match the treatment method to the threat and the equipment.

  1. Use a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes at elevations above 6,500 feet, when treating microbiologically unsafe clear water.
  2. Let water cool in a clean container with a secure cover.
  3. If boiling is not possible, use a suitable filter then a disinfection method as directed by its manufacturer.
  4. Keep dirty and treated water bottles, hoses, and collection surfaces separate.
  5. Replace or backflush a filter according to its manual; freezing can damage some filters.

Safety: No field method here makes water with toxic chemical, fuel, or radioactive contamination safe.

Shelter

Emergency warmth without a campfire

Dry layers and insulation from the ground can matter more than flame.

  1. Move out of wind and water to a location free of falling trees, flood risk, and rockfall.
  2. Put on dry insulation and rain/wind protection; replace wet layers when possible.
  3. Use a sleeping pad, pack, or other insulating barrier between you and the ground.
  4. Use a bivy, space blanket, or tarp to reduce wind and precipitation exposure while maintaining ventilation.
  5. Minimize exertion that causes sweating and monitor companions for worsening cold stress.

Safety: Do not use fuel-burning heaters or stoves inside tents or enclosed shelters; carbon monoxide can kill.

Shelter

Tarp shelter setup: safe principles

A taut, well-sited tarp protects against wind and rain.

  1. Pick a location away from drainage channels, dead branches, flood-prone flats, and isolated exposed ridges.
  2. Orient the low side toward the prevailing wind and allow runoff away from sleeping gear.
  3. Use stable anchors and avoid damaging live trees.
  4. Tension ridge and corner lines using knots appropriate to the load; protect cords from abrasion.
  5. Keep the shelter low enough to reduce wind but ventilated enough to prevent condensation.
  6. Check tension and drainage after conditions change.

Safety: Avoid any shelter location near falling deadwood (“widowmakers”).

Shelter

Sleep system: pad, bag, and moisture

A sleeping bag alone is not enough on cold ground.

  1. Choose an insulation system suited to forecast temperatures, precipitation, and wind.
  2. Put a groundsheet or appropriate tent floor under gear; prevent pooling runoff.
  3. Use an insulating pad below the sleeping bag.
  4. Keep the sleeping bag dry and change out of damp clothes before sleeping when possible.
  5. Vent moisture and dry gear whenever safe conditions allow.

Navigation

Orient a map to north

Make the terrain and printed map face the same way.

  1. Find north on the map and check its declination diagram and date.
  2. Keep the compass away from phones, magnets, metal, and electrical equipment that can disturb readings.
  3. Lay the compass on a flat map and align the compass housing with map north.
  4. Rotate map and compass together until the needle aligns with the correct north reference.
  5. Compare major terrain features to confirm orientation before choosing a route.

Safety: Magnetic north differs from true north, and the difference changes by place and time; use current local declination.

NPS navigation preparedness Source online
Navigation

Compass bearings: practice safely

Practice route bearings in a known safe area before relying on them.

  1. Select a clear, visible target on safe accessible terrain.
  2. Hold the compass level and away from nearby magnetic interference.
  3. Rotate the housing to match the desired bearing, taking account of local declination as appropriate.
  4. Turn your body until the needle aligns with the housing orientation marks.
  5. Identify an intermediate landmark on that bearing and walk to it; repeat instead of staring at the compass while moving.
  6. Cross-check with the map, terrain, and known checkpoints.

Safety: A bearing does not override terrain hazards; never follow a line blindly into cliffs or water.

Contours, slopes, and terrain traps

Topographic maps show steepness and landforms.

  1. Read the legend to find contour interval and map scale.
  2. Closely spaced contour lines indicate steeper slopes; wide spacing indicates gentler ground.
  3. Use saddles, ridgelines, drainages, and summits as recognizable terrain features.
  4. Remember that thick forest, cliffs, snow, and water crossings can make a short line impassable.
  5. Choose routes that match ability, daylight, weather, and access restrictions.

Coordinates and your location

Record your position before an emergency.

  1. When GPS is available, wait for a reliable position fix in open sky when possible.

Weather

Reading changing weather

Field observations support—but cannot replace—a forecast.

  1. Check the latest official forecast and warnings before departure while connected.
  2. Watch for quickly growing thunderstorm clouds, increasing wind, abrupt cooling, or darkening skies.
  3. Monitor visibility, temperature, precipitation, and nearby drainage changes.
  4. Turn toward safe shelter or the trailhead before conditions deteriorate.
  5. Avoid exposed ridges and water when storms threaten.

Safety: Cloud shape alone cannot reliably predict a safe weather window.

Weather

Thunderstorm decisions

If you can hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike.

  1. Plan an exit to a substantial enclosed building or hard-topped vehicle before storms approach.
  2. If thunder is heard, go to proper shelter immediately; tents and open-sided shelters do not protect against lightning.
  3. Stay off ridges, away from water, and out from under isolated trees while seeking shelter.
  4. Remain inside safe shelter for at least 30 minutes after the last thunder.
  5. If no safe shelter is available, move away from the highest and most exposed terrain but understand there is no safe outdoor location.

Campcraft

Five camp knots and where they fit

Know what each knot is for—and what it is not for.

  1. Bowline: forms a fixed loop; learn to dress and secure it correctly.
  2. Clove hitch: convenient temporary attachment to a post; may loosen if load changes.
  3. Two half hitches: common method to secure a line to a ring or tree.
  4. Taut-line hitch: adjustable tension for some tent and tarp guy lines.
  5. Figure-eight stopper: helps keep a line from passing through a small opening.
  6. Practice tying, dressing, loading gently, and untying all five in a controlled setting.

Safety: These descriptions are not climbing or life-safety knot instruction. Never rely on a recreational knot for rescue, climbing, or fall protection.
